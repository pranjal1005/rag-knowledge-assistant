ETL vs ELT: Two Patterns for Moving Data
ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) and ELT (Extract, Load, Transform) describe the order in which raw data is moved into a destination system and reshaped into a usable form. In ETL, data is extracted from a source system, transformed in a separate processing layer (cleaning, joining, aggregating), and only then loaded into the target warehouse. This was the dominant pattern when warehouse compute was expensive and limited, so transformation happened on dedicated servers before the warehouse ever saw the data.

ELT reverses the last two steps: raw data is loaded into the warehouse first, and transformation happens afterward using the warehouse's own compute (SQL, dbt models, etc.). This became practical once cloud warehouses like Snowflake, BigQuery, and Redshift made compute cheap and elastic. The main advantages of ELT are that raw data is preserved for reprocessing if transformation logic changes, and transformations can be version-controlled as SQL rather than opaque ETL-tool pipelines.

The tradeoff is governance and cost: ELT means untransformed, potentially sensitive raw data lands in the warehouse before any masking or filtering happens, which has compliance implications, and warehouse compute for transformation is billed per-query rather than amortized on a fixed ETL server. In practice, most modern data platforms use a hybrid: light extraction and format normalization on the way in (ETL for schema and PII handling), followed by ELT-style modeling inside the warehouse for business logic.
